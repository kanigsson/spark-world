 What was your biggest hurdle in your task, both in general and specific to
gnatprove usage?

Honest answer on both counts:

In general: designing the code so that safety is locally provable, before
writing any of it. The actual decoding logic was the easy part — puff.c is a
known quantity. The hard work was choosing representations where every array
index and every addition has a bound the prover can see from nearby facts
rather than from a whole-program argument. Concretely: making Consumed/Produced
relative offsets instead of absolute indices (so bounds are <= Input'Length,
not relations between 'First and 'Last that overflow at the edges), capping
Buffer_Index at Integer'Last - 1 so "one past the end" is always computable,
taking Output as in out to sidestep Relaxed_Initialization proofs, and deciding
in advance which bounds to prove properly versus guard defensively. The
Huffman-table slot bound is the example: "offsets never exceed 288" is true
because counts sum to the number of nonzero lengths, but proving that needs
ghost summation and induction lemmas. Spending a free runtime check to turn an
unreachable state into a defined error status kept the whole proof at level 2
with no manual lemmas. Choosing where to make that trade is engineering judgment
the prover can't make for you.

A close second: test-oracle subtlety. My only test failure all session
was my own wrong expectation (a force_zip64 archive that is legitimately
classic-readable), and ZIP mutations needed a "no oracle, just must-not-crash"
category because Python's zipfile is stricter than us in some ways (it compares
local vs. central names) and looser in others. Differential testing is only as
good as your understanding of the reference's quirks.

Specific to gnatprove, two things:

1. Variable-exponent powers. Every initially-unproved check (5 of 261 on the
first run) traced to one root: 2 ** N with a symbolic exponent. The provers
wouldn't connect Bit_Buf and (Shift_Left(1, N) - 1) to < 2**N, and wouldn't
apply power monotonicity (Len <= 15 ⟹ 2**Len <= 2**15) for the overflow checks
in Decode. The fix was to banish symbolic powers entirely: Mask_Table and Pow2
as concrete constant arrays, which the provers handle by case enumeration over
the aggregate axioms. After that substitution, nothing power-related ever failed
again — including the later Build_Fast stride loop, where writing the fill as
J := J + Pow2(Len) instead of computing 2**(10-Len) repetitions made the bound
proof trivial and avoided needing 2**a * 2**b = 2**(a+b).
2. Invariants that are true but not inductive. This is the classic trap and
I walked into it twice. Code < 2**Len in the bit-by-bit decoder is true at
every loop top, but the preservation VC fails without also carrying Code mod
2 = 0 — an odd Code at the bound would double past it, even though the program
never produces one. Conversely in Build_Fast I had to weaken < to <= because a
complete code exactly fills its space. Relatedly, termination-by-default meant
every postcondition had to say the right thing about progress: Bit_Position
(consumed bits) as the single global measure worked beautifully, but the
strict-versus-non-strict bookkeeping had to be exact — Stored can't promise
strict increase on its truncation path, GZip.Decompress needed Status = OK
⟹ Consumed > 0 added just so Decompress_All's variant would discharge. The
pleasant surprise was the payoff for getting this right: First <= Code turned
out to be the inductive invariant that made the decoder's overflow bounds fall
out for free (replacing an ugly 577 * 2**Len - 576 bound I'd derived on paper),
and the fully optimized decoder re-proved on its first run.

What was not a hurdle, notably: prover capacity. At --level=2 nothing needed
more than a second, no manual Why3 sessions, no pragma Assume. The friction was
entirely in phrasing, never in solving.
